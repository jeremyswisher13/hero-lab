Cold water immersion and recovery

Evidence on soreness, performance, sleep, and resistance-training adaptation.

Does cold water immersion help recovery?

Cold water immersion can reduce perceived soreness after strenuous exercise compared with passive recovery. That does not mean every performance measure improves. Findings depend on the exercise, comparison, timing, and outcome. Sleep and long-term muscle adaptation need separate consideration.

This is a HERO Lab evidence review. It is not an American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) position statement; HERO’s AMSSM conference presentation is a separate research source.

Recovery depends on what you measure

Muscle soreness
Some pooled studies show less perceived soreness, including at 24 hours after high-intensity exercise. Effects vary with exercise type and time. Review the evidence.
Subsequent performance
Small next-day power benefits appear in some studies; strength and endurance effects are less consistent. Jump performance can decrease immediately after cooling. See the timing limits.
Sleep
Small athlete trials report mixed findings. A reliable benefit for whole-night sleep has not been established. Read the sleep studies.
Muscle growth
Repeated CWI immediately after resistance training may modestly reduce muscle growth. Occasional or delayed use remains less certain. Read the adaptation review.

Published evidence and original sources

These are selected studies, not an exhaustive systematic review. Protocols describe research conditions, not a personal ice-bath prescription.

2022Sports Medicine

Impact of Cold-Water Immersion Compared with Passive Recovery

Moore E, Fuller JT, Buckley JD, et al.

A systematic review of 52 studies compared CWI with passive recovery after one strenuous exercise bout in physically active adults. Benefits varied by exercise type and assessment time. Some soreness and muscular-power outcomes favored CWI around 24 hours; pooled strength and endurance recovery did not clearly improve.

How to interpret it: The review studied CWI begun within 15 minutes after exercise. Differences in temperature, duration, and exercise do not establish one optimal protocol for every athlete.

PubMed Open full text DOI: 10.1007/s40279-022-01644-9
2023Sports Medicine

Effects of Cold-Water Immersion Compared with Other Recovery Modalities

Moore E, Fuller JT, Bellenger CR, et al.

This review included 28 studies comparing CWI with other recovery methods. Soreness recovery favored CWI, while power and flexibility outcomes were broadly similar. Air cryotherapy performed better for strength recovery and power at one hour.

How to interpret it: CWI was not superior for every outcome. The comparator and assessment time matter. The linked publisher page provides the abstract and full-text access options.

PubMed Publisher / access options DOI: 10.1007/s40279-022-01800-1
2023Frontiers in Physiology

Effects of cold water immersion after exercise on fatigue recovery and exercise performance

Xiao F, Kabachkova AV, Jiao L, Zhao H, Kapilevich LV.

Across 20 studies, pooled soreness and perceived exertion decreased immediately after treatment, but countermovement-jump performance also decreased at that time. Some later muscle-damage and metabolic markers favored CWI.

How to interpret it: Feeling less sore, changing a blood marker, and performing better are different outcomes. This analysis does not justify a general claim that immediate CWI improves athletic performance.

PubMed Open full text
2013 & 2021Athlete sleep trials

Cold water immersion and sleep: two small athlete trials

Robey E, Dawson B, Halson S, et al. (2013); Chauvineau M, Pasquier F, Guyot V, et al. (2021).

Robey and colleagues studied 11 male cyclists with laboratory polysomnography and found no whole-night sleep differences between conditions. Chauvineau and colleagues studied 12 trained male runners and found selected changes in sleep architecture under a specific immersion protocol, without clear fatigue or muscle-damage benefits over 48 hours.

How to interpret it: These small studies used different protocols. They do not establish a reliable general improvement in total sleep or overall recovery.

Robey: PubMed Robey: publisher / DOI Chauvineau: PubMed Chauvineau: open full text
2024European Journal of Sport Science

Throwing cold water on muscle growth

Piñero A, Burke R, Augustin F, et al.

A systematic review with meta-analysis of eight interventions found that repeated CWI after resistance exercise may modestly attenuate muscle growth. The studies lasted 4–12 weeks and involved almost entirely young men; the underlying study quality was fair to poor.

How to interpret it: Participants could still gain muscle. Most protocols used CWI after every resistance session, soon after exercise. The review does not establish the effects of occasional use or immersion several hours later.

Open full text DOI: 10.1002/ejsc.12074
