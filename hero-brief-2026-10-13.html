What we're working on

Magnesium, three ways.

Our randomized, blinded study in UCLA varsity athletes is now underway, comparing magnesium glycinate, magnesium L-threonate, and placebo. The protocol includes one week of tracking before four weeks on assigned capsules. The main question is whether either form changes WHOOP-estimated sleep efficiency, the proportion of time in bed spent asleep, from the baseline week to the final treatment week. We are also measuring reaction time, grip strength, and jump performance. Results are not available yet. Study registration →

Travel, with a plan.

Our review in Sports Medicine pulls together what helps athletes arrive ready to compete. Three things to take with you:

  • Know which problem you have. Travel fatigue is the wear and tear of the trip. Jet lag is your body clock disagreeing with the destination. They get different fixes.
  • Shift early, do not shorten. When a timezone adjustment is appropriate, consider moving sleep and wake times toward the destination by 30 to 60 minutes a day, 3 to 7 days before departure, without shortening sleep.
  • Plan caffeine around bedtime. The review suggests avoiding caffeine within about 8 to 10 hours of target bedtime. Coffee, energy drinks, and pre-workout all count toward the same total.

Three worth your time

Recovery methods versus rest. A review of 16 randomized trials involving 273 endurance athletes found no robust evidence that the tested recovery methods outperformed passive rest. Most comparisons had low or very low certainty, so the findings do not prove every method ineffective. Frontiers in Sports and Active Living →

Contrast showers, four weeks in. In a trial of 60 combat-sport athletes, four weeks of post-training contrast showers produced favorable changes in resting heart-rate variability and perceived recovery compared with thermoneutral showers. Those differences were not maintained after a two-week washout. Journal of Sports Science and Medicine →

Yesterday's running, tonight's recovery. Across one collegiate season, researchers followed 26 women's soccer players. Previous-day high-speed running was associated with overnight recovery, next-day readiness, and maximum speed. Models using high-speed running performed better than models using total distance. This observational study does not establish causation. International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance →

Worth a look

Eight Sleep Pod 6: what changed?

Eight Sleep introduced Pod 6 in September. Here are the details that matter if temperature is disrupting your sleep:

  • What's new. Compared with Pod 5, Eight Sleep reports 20% faster temperature response, a hub about half the size, and nine biometric sensors per side instead of one. The extra sensors are intended to better separate partners' signals when they sleep close together. These are manufacturer claims. What changed →
  • Fit and upgrades. The cover goes over an existing mattress, with separate temperature control for each sleeper. New Solo versions fit Twin/Twin XL or one side of a larger bed. Pod 6 requires its own cover and hub together; neither can be mixed with earlier generations. Sizes → · Compatibility →
  • Budget for the membership. Solo hardware starts at $1,999. Autopilot starts at $199 annually and is required for the first 12 months, putting the advertised entry cost at $2,198 before tax for the first year. That is Solo pricing; check your mattress size and configuration. Plans → · First-year requirement →

What the research shows. A small, nonrandomized study followed 32 male Australian-rules footballers for at least two weeks. Pod 3 users reported better sleep duration and quality than controls using their usual bedding. These preliminary results need confirmation in randomized research with validated objective measures; they do not establish a sleep or performance benefit for Pod 6. Read the study →
